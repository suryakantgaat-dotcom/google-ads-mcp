# Build authority on LinkedIn with proprietary evidence

**Scope caveat (revised 2026-11-20): a verification pass read these on primary pages: LinkedIn's own pages (news.linkedin.com, LinkedIn Engineering, Help Center), arXiv, Wikipedia, Originality.ai, Buffer, Sprout Social, Socialinsider, AuthoredUp, Hootsuite and Metricool. These were blocked (403 or proxy refusal) and not read: Edelman, Search Engine Land, PPC Land, Fast Company, Social Media Today, ContentGrip and linkedin.com/pulse. Claims resting only on blocked sources keep their old evidence level (search-result summaries, second-hand) and are listed under "Open gaps". Sources outside the verification pass (legal commentary, practitioner blogs, Ordinal, MagicPost, LigoSocial, Magnettu, DEV, Supergrow and similar) also remain at their old level. Many sources are vendors selling a product, and Socialinsider's PDFs and Metricool's full study were not retrievable.** With that limit, the best-supported rule for a Google/Meta performance marketer building authority is to publish first-party evidence: real account experiments, teardowns, and measurement judgments with baselines, caveats, and honest nulls. Buyers are reported to say that demonstrating understanding of their problems builds trust more than product strength (56% vs 14%), and 84% to use AI to summarize thought leadership, which would make generic advice a commodity and proprietary data the scarce asset ([Edelman 2025, not read](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report); [ContentGrip, not read](https://www.contentgrip.com/edelman-ai-thought-leadership-buyers/)). LinkedIn's March 2026 newsroom post says it is reducing generic, recycled, repetitive, low-substance posts and engagement bait, and that the change "isn't about limiting reach or discouraging participation" ([LinkedIn News](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed), read). That post never mentions AI, so "LinkedIn penalizes AI-written content" is unverified; the stated target is quality and bait, not authorship. None of the LinkedIn pages read states numeric algorithm folklore (golden hour, hashtag counts, comment multipliers, link penalties, 360Brew, edit effects). No source studied hiring managers, so that audience rests on inference.

Evidence key used throughout: **A** = stated by LinkedIn or in a peer-reviewable paper, and the primary page was read; **B** = large third-party dataset, usually vendor-run, primary page read unless marked "not read"; **C** = practitioner opinion, small/single-account test, or a primary page read that only supports the point loosely (for example Wikipedia's encyclopedic guidance); **I** = my inference from sources; **[Unverified]** = no traceable source or not found on the primary page.

## Original evidence is the scarce asset buyers reward

All Edelman figures in this section are unread (edelman.com, PPC Land, ContentGrip and Search Engine Land were blocked) and stay at their old evidence level. Edelman-LinkedIn's 2025 study (1,934 executives, per search summary) reportedly found 79% more likely to champion a vendor in an RFP if it consistently publishes quality thought leadership, and 59% of final decision-makers said weak or absent thought leadership contributed to a vendor being dropped (cited to the 2026 page; edition and base unconfirmed) ([Edelman 2026 page, not read](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2026-b2b-thought-leadership-report)). The 2026 edition (about 1,135 respondents) reportedly says 82% say thought leadership gains value as AI spreads, and buyers prize original data and human judgment AI answers lack ([PPC Land, not read](https://ppc.land/82-of-b2b-buyers-value-thought-leadership-more-as-ai-spreads-edelman-finds/)). Critics reportedly note the study does not show causation and that research is only original if you do not know the answer at the start ([ContentGrip](https://www.contentgrip.com/edelman-ai-thought-leadership-buyers/); [Gaskell](https://adigaskell.substack.com/p/edelman-report-downplays-the-strategic)). Earlier notes also flag conflicting "72-79%" and "71% vs 75%" figures, unresolved. Edelman covers buyers, not recruiters; evidence on how hiring managers weigh LinkedIn content versus portfolios was not found.

Practitioner sources converge on a defensible point of view: one narrow, non-obvious position backed by first-hand cases, and contrarian only if it "rings true" ([Animalz](https://www.animalz.co/blog/thought-leadership-content/); [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/position-yourself-as-a-thought-leader/)). Search Engine Land experiment write-ups (not read) supply the norms for credible performance posts: a hypothesis, one isolated variable, enough conversions per variant, 1-2 week learning periods, and published non-significant results ([SEL](https://searchengineland.com/ppc-experimentation-vs-ppc-testing-448771); [SEL null-result case](https://searchengineland.com/a-case-study-for-delivering-performance-in-a-mature-google-ads-account-315176)). The experiment template (hypothesis, setup, baseline, result with significance, what you would change) is my inference from those norms (I).

Client results carry legal risk that platform policy does not. NDAs often bar disclosing even the existence of an engagement, and written approval is the recommended standard for metrics ([Terms.law](https://terms.law/NDA/scenarios/professional-services/agency-services-nda.html); [Mario Peshev](https://mariopeshev.com/answers/case-studies-digital-agencies/)); agencies have been forced to remove unapproved case studies ([Campaign](https://campaignlive.com/article/name-shame-why-industry-names-favorite-brands-cant-talk/1968948)). The FTC rule on reviews and testimonials (effective 21 Oct 2024) requires disclosure of generally expected results for atypical outcomes; "results not typical" disclaimers do not suffice ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/08/ftc-issues-final-rule-on-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials)). These legal sources were outside the verification pass. No official Google or Meta policy on publishing case studies was found.

## Formats and structures: documents lead, structure advice is opinion

Document (PDF carousel) posts lead engagement rate on three primary pages read. Socialinsider (company pages only; methodology: 2,450,610 organic posts from about 26,000 pages with 1K-1M followers; its intro still says 1.3M posts, an inconsistency on the page) reports 2025 engagement by impressions of 7.00% for native documents, 6.45% multi-image, 6.00% video, 5.30% image, 4.50% text, 4.20% poll, 3.25% link; overall average 5.20%. A later quarterly update on the same page shows Q3 2026 documents and multi-image tied at 6.40%, video 5.25%, text 3.50%, poll 3.60%, link 2.50% ([Socialinsider benchmarks](https://www.socialinsider.io/social-media-benchmarks/linkedin); the PDFs were not retrievable). AuthoredUp (personal profiles only, 3M+ posts, March 2025-February 2026, multipliers relative to each profile's own median) gives reach / engagement of 1.39x / 1.30x for documents, 1.20x / 1.33x image, 1.07x / 0.78x text-only, 0.86x / 0.93x video, 0.69x / 0.44x article, and 1.78x / 0.37x for polls, which it calls a "reach trap" ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/best-performing-content-on-linkedin)). Buffer's frequency page reports median engagement against text of about 72% more for images, 84% more for video, and carousels "nearly 600%" more than text, with link posts lowest ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-to-post-on-linkedin/)). Video is mixed: Socialinsider and Buffer show it above text on engagement, while AuthoredUp gives it 0.86x reach and both report video reach or views down 36% year on year. Metricool's 2026 pages say carousels get 17x more interactions than images and questions get 77% more comments ([Metricool](https://metricool.com/linkedin-trends-study/); full study gated); the earlier Metricool figures (poll reach +206%, carousel about 46% above other formats) do not appear on its 2026 pages and are [Unverified]. LigoSocial's polls at -50% engagement was not read. Socialinsider is company-page data, so rates may not transfer to a personal profile (I).

For length, AuthoredUp's 372,126 personal-profile posts (Sep 2025-Feb 2026) show median engagement of 2.10% under 400 characters rising to 2.61% at 1,301-2,000 and 2.67% at 2,001-2,500, a 27% lift for 1,301-2,500 over under 400 ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-character-limit)). That lift is a raw median comparison and long posts lean toward bigger accounts: compared against each author's own usual reach (709,195 posts, Jul 2025-Jun 2026), differences between lengths shrink to "a few percent". Text-only posts under 400 characters still land last (about 14% below the author's usual reach), and text does best around 1,200-1,500 characters. Magnettu's 800-1,500 was not read. The post limit is 3,000 characters. No head-to-head study of structures (case study, teardown, contrarian, framework) exists; the sources only recommend a specific-number or contrarian hook, evidence-backed body, and a discussion-oriented close ([GTM Stack](https://gtmstack-production.pages.dev/playbooks/linkedin-posting-playbook), not read). No source names verified performance-marketing creators or their post data. Newsletter-versus-post reach data does not exist in these sources (AuthoredUp gives articles 0.69x reach and 0.44x engagement, but that is LinkedIn articles on personal profiles).

## AI-slop tells are structural, and the penalty is perceived inauthenticity

Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" is a WikiProject AI Cleanup advice page, "descriptive, not prescriptive", written to detect undisclosed AI text on Wikipedia, with some signs that "may not apply in a non-Wikipedia context" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), read as raw wikitext). It says not all text with these signs is AI-generated, that humans and detectors are both unreliable, and: "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder." So the list is a diagnostic for editing, not a recipe for passing as human, and substance matters more than scrubbing. Structure is better supported than single words: Kobak et al. (Science Advances, 2025) analyzed 15.1M English PubMed abstracts from 2010-2024 and estimated at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed (up to 40% in some subcorpora); excess words included "delves" (r = 28.0), "underscores" and "showcasing", and, among common words, "potential", "findings" and "crucial". The domain is biomedical abstracts, not LinkedIn ([arXiv 2406.07016v5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016v5)). Word lists date fast: Wikipedia says "delve" peaked in 2023-early 2024 and dropped off sharply in 2025, and lists different words for each model era through mid-2026 (for example "leverage", "multifaceted", "prioritize"). Perception research supports the authenticity risk: in a 261-person study (990 responses; texts were all GPT-4o-generated and participants were deceived about the AI share), disclosed AI authorship eroded perceived trustworthiness, caring, competence and likability, mitigated by higher AI literacy ([arXiv 2510.24011](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24011), accepted at IUI '26), and a controlled experiment with 1,970 human raters of one news article found disclosed AI use was penalized by human and LLM raters ([arXiv 2507.01418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01418)). Both are analogies, not LinkedIn data.

Engagement evidence is thin and conflicting. Originality.ai's current page reports 81.2% (4,061 of 5,000) of public long-form LinkedIn posts of 100+ words sampled in July 2026 classified "Likely AI" at a 15% AI Allowance; it covers only posts found by its searches and is a detector label, not proof of machine authorship ([Originality.ai](https://originality.ai/blog/ai-content-published-linkedin)). The older "about 54% of 8,795 posts" figure no longer appears on that page, and "45% less engagement" never appears there (the page has no engagement analysis); both are secondary-only [Unverified], as are the PPC Land figure of AI-draft human-edited posts at 6.85% versus 6.22% for purely human (blocked) and Pangram's 41% ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/over-half-of-linkedin-posts-are-now-likely-ai-but-authenticity-still-wins/)).

LinkedIn's stated test, on pages read, is substance and authenticity: fewer "repetitive, low-substance posts and engagement bait" and more posts that "offer genuine insight, actionable ideas, and thoughtful perspectives" ([LinkedIn News](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed)). The Fast Company line that AI-assisted posts are welcome with original ideas could not be read. A "Seems like AI slop" report option dated 30 July 2026 is reported on Originality.ai's page, which quotes LinkedIn's Chief Product Officer and says LinkedIn has not said whether it is available globally; it was not found on any LinkedIn page read (C, vendor-reported). The 94% detection and 40% fewer views figures stay [Unverified]. Credible-writer technique converges on supplying your own specifics, voice samples, and numbers (the model may invent a number you do not provide), and editing clusters of tells rather than banning single words ([Your AI Copyeditor](https://youraicopyeditor.substack.com/p/stop-your-writing-from-sounding-like); [PR Daily](https://www.prdaily.com/4-reasons-your-writing-accidentally-sounds-ai-generated-and-how-to-fix-it/); [Bea Krause Sorio](https://beakrausesorio.substack.com/p/how-to-write-well-with-ai-without)); these were not re-read. No controlled evidence shows any technique raises LinkedIn performance.

## Algorithm claims: only pods, comment automation, bait, and generic content have official backing

LinkedIn's Engineering post of 12 March 2026 describes LLM-based retrieval replacing several heterogeneous retrieval sources, and a Generative Recommender that treats a member's interaction history as a sequence (more than a thousand interactions), with long dwells, likes, comments and shares as modeled actions ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/engineering-the-next-generation-of-linkedins-feed), read). The newsroom post of the same date says LinkedIn is working to make engagement pods ineffective, curb comment automation and third-party tools that create fake conversations, and reduce "comment to agree" prompts and videos that do not match the text ([LinkedIn News](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed)). Companion arXiv papers describe the ranker (Feed SR, replacing a DCNv2 ranker; +2.10% time spent, +3.52% likes, comments or reshares in A/B tests; [2602.12354](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12354)), retrieval of suggested content (LLaMA 3 dual encoder; [2510.14223](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14223)) and LiGR ([2502.03417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03417)). The Help Center lists relevance signals including what a post is about, recency, whether it comes from someone you follow, the language, how constructive the conversation is, tagged people, companies or topics, and time spent viewing, and says low-quality content is filtered or tapered ([Help Center](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339724)). LinkedIn has not published ranking weights, link penalties, hashtag counts, a golden hour, or posting frequency guidance on any page read. The table grades the popular claims.

## Cadence, hooks, CTAs, and commenting rest on vendor data

Frequency and timing evidence conflicts (see table). Buffer's within-account regression favors more posting with diminishing returns; AuthoredUp's personal-profile medians peak at 4-5 per week; Metricool describes about 3 per week for personal profiles. Account mix, metric definitions and sample designs differ, so treat cadence and timing as hypotheses to test against the user's own analytics. Ordinal's analysis of per-post reach falling gently as frequency rises was not re-read ([Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-posting-frequency)). Best-time sources do not agree: Buffer 3-8 pm, Hootsuite early morning, Metricool about 10-11 am, Sprout midday. Sprout's sample is cross-network and mostly brands (I).

The "see more" fold is about 210 characters on desktop and about 140 on mobile, as observed by AuthoredUp, not specified by LinkedIn ([AuthoredUp](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-character-limit)). Hook data conflicts and was not re-read: AuthoredUp hook medians (story 2.60%, contrarian 2.31%, question 2.16%) come via [Ordinal](https://www.tryordinal.com/blog/linkedin-hooks) and were not found on the AuthoredUp pages fetched; a ConnectSafely sample of 1,000+ posts puts curiosity-gap and contrarian highest and questions lowest ([DEV](https://dev.to/belal_zahran/i-analyzed-1000-viral-linkedin-posts-heres-the-formula-5a3o)); Ciela found 94% of viral posts had a hook with a specific number among four elements ([Ciela](https://www.ciela.ai/blogs/viral-linkedin-posts-ai-analysis)). A CTA-led first line is among the worst ([Supergrow](https://growthtools.supergrow.ai/linkedin-growth-playbook)). Engagement-rate definitions differ, so figures are not comparable across sources. Buffer's 58-post single-account experiment (60 days, 568,000 impressions) gives no hook statistics ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/linkedin-experiment/)).

On CTAs, LinkedIn names "comment to agree" prompts as low-value content ([LinkedIn News](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed)), and its spam policy says it may limit distribution of content designed to artificially increase engagement, listing emoji/reaction polls that artificially boost engagement and chain-letter requests for likes, reactions and shares ([Help Center](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1338787)). A 2023 change reducing visibility for posts explicitly asking for reactions and bait polls is reported by SEL (blocked, not read) ([SEL](https://searchengineland.com/linkedin-feed-less-polls-engagement-bait-384986)). "Comment X" DM-gated funnels are called flagged by marketing blogs, not confirmed by LinkedIn ([LinkedInPreview](https://linkedinpreview.com/blog/linkedin-engagement-bait-2026)). For commenting, the author replying in the first hour correlates with 67% more engagement in an unreviewed 50,000-post analysis ([DEV](https://dev.to/olams/i-analyzed-50000-linkedin-posts-heres-what-actually-gets-engagement-56l9)); other vendor figures (comment in first 30 minutes gets about 4x impressions; replies lift a comment up to 2.4x) are [Unverified] (Hootsuite's comment article was not fetched) ([Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/long-vs-short-linkedin-comments/)). LinkedIn's confirmed target is comment automation and third-party tools that create fake conversations; the Entrepreneur claim about AI-generated comments specifically was not read ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/linkedin-is-fighting-back-against-ai-slop-and-ai-comments)). Claims of 3x inbound from commenting are anecdotal ([StartupGTM](https://startupgtm.substack.com/p/comment-os)). Profile-optimization statistics (for example 21x views with a photo) have no original study [Unverified].

## Skill-ready rule set

## Conclusion

The sourced picture is narrower than LinkedIn advice usually claims. The only rules with platform-level backing, now read on LinkedIn's own pages, are to supply specific, substantive content and to avoid pods, comment automation, engagement bait and generic recycled posts; whether AI authorship itself is penalized is unverified, and the buyer-survey support for original data remains unread. Format, length, cadence, timing and hook numbers are vendor correlations that disagree across sources (timing and frequency conflict outright; the length lift largely disappears within-author), so a skill should present them as defaults to test against the user's own analytics. Wikipedia's own warning that fixing listed signs alone can make detection harder points the same way. The most useful design implication is that the skill's main job is intake rather than generation: it should collect the user's real data, baseline, caveats, and client-permission status first, then draft.
